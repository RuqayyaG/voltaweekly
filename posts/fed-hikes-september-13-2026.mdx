The Federal Reserve raised rates by 25 basis points today, bringing the federal funds rate to a target range of 3.75 to 4.00%. The vote was not unanimous. Going into the meeting, futures markets had the probability of a hike at 92.7%.

The context is unusual by any measure. Brent crude is above $107. The Saudi East-West pipeline has been shut since September 11 after drone strikes from Iraq. The 10-year Treasury yield is sitting at 4.98%. The labour market lost 23,000 jobs in July. On the employment numbers alone, this looked like a hold.

The case for hiking anyway comes down to one thing: credibility.

The Fed was late in 2021. It called inflation transitory while it wasn't and has been trying to rebuild its inflation-fighting reputation ever since. Kevin Warsh, who took over as chair, has been more hawkish in tone than his predecessor. Three members dissented in July in favour of an immediate hike. When the minutes from that meeting came out in August, they showed broader concern that waiting further risked letting inflation expectations drift.

## The argument against, and why it probably lost

Hiking into a supply shock is textbook bad monetary policy. Rate increases reduce demand. They do not fix a closed strait or a shut pipeline. Raising borrowing costs when American consumers are already paying well over $4 a gallon for petrol risks tipping a weakening labour market into something worse.

But the counterargument runs like this: the oil shock is feeding into core goods prices through transport costs, manufacturing inputs, and anything that moves on a truck. July CPI was 3.4% and core was 2.5%. Neither is catastrophic, but core at 2.5% with oil up 22% in a single month means September and October inflation prints are going to be written in real time by what happens to energy prices over the next few weeks.

The Fed's dot plot from June showed nine members projecting at least one hike in 2026 while eight projected no change. That split reflects a genuine debate, not a foregone conclusion. A CNBC survey of economists found respondents now see two hikes ahead rather than one, driven entirely by the energy outlook rather than by growth or employment.

## What it means from here

The dollar firmed into the meeting. Emerging markets with dollar-denominated debt are under pressure. Countries that import most of their energy and borrow in dollars are being squeezed from both ends. Turkey, India, South Africa are the obvious names.

For the UK, the Bank of England is watching closely. The MPC meets next week. The ECB already hiked 25 basis points on September 10. The pattern of developed market central banks tightening into an energy supply shock is now established. The question is whether tighter monetary policy makes the eventual landing harder or whether letting inflation expectations slip makes it harder still.

There is no clean answer. The Fed chose credibility over caution today. Whether that was the right call will depend on whether the pipeline comes back online and whether the Strait situation has any diplomatic resolution in sight. Neither looks imminent.

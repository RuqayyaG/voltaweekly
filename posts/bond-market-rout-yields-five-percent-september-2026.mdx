The US 10-year Treasury yield hit 5.23% on Thursday, its highest level in nearly two decades. The 30-year touched levels not seen since 2004. By Friday it had eased back to 5.17% as oil fell on Middle East peace speculation, but the weekly move was significant. Over the past month the 10-year is up 51 basis points. Over the past year it's up 99.

The bond market is pricing something serious. Equity markets, broadly, are not. The S&P 500 finished the week in positive territory. The VIX fell 6.5% to 14.81. A VIX at 14 and a 10-year at 5.17% existing at the same time is historically unusual. It probably doesn't last.

## What's driving yields

Three things are running simultaneously and pointing the same way.

The Fed hiked on September 16, taking the federal funds rate to 3.75 to 4.00%. Kevin Warsh's subsequent commentary suggests another hike before year end is on the table. Higher short rates keep pressure on the entire curve.

Then there's energy. Oil above $100 feeds into headline CPI with a lag. September and October inflation prints will reflect energy costs that were significantly elevated from the June base. The market is pricing this through longer-dated bonds. Real yields, the return on Treasuries after stripping out inflation expectations, are rising alongside nominal yields. That makes this more than just an inflation story. Investors are demanding more compensation for holding long-duration US debt regardless of what inflation does.

Finally, supply. The US Treasury is issuing a lot of bonds. The Fed is simultaneously running down its balance sheet through quantitative tightening, removing a buyer that had previously absorbed significant issuance. More supply, one fewer major buyer, prices fall and yields rise. That dynamic doesn't resolve quickly.

## Why equities haven't followed

Higher yields should hurt stocks through two channels. The discount rate on future earnings rises, making companies worth less in present value terms today. And higher borrowing costs squeeze corporate margins over time. Both are real. Neither has been the dominant story for equities yet.

The reason is earnings have held up. AI-related capital spending has kept technology investment high. The labour market, while softening, hasn't broken. Consumers are still spending despite higher fuel costs. Stocks are broadly pricing a scenario where inflation stays awkwardly elevated but nothing actually breaks. That's uncomfortable, but the equity market has decided it's survivable.

The problem with that view is timing. The Fed started hiking in 2022. The housing market cracked in 2023. Corporate defaults have been rising slowly through 2025 and into 2026. The economy has been absorbing rate shock for four years. Each additional move higher in the 10-year adds to a load that was already heavy before September's hike.

A CNBC survey of economists now expects two more hikes rather than one. If the 10-year settles above 5.25% rather than pulling back, the question of what that does to equity valuations stops being theoretical. JPMorgan and several other banks have already flagged it.

Oil fell nearly 4% this week despite active pipeline disruptions and the Hormuz situation. The tape ignored the headlines. If energy keeps softening while bond yields stay at these levels, something somewhere is mispriced. Markets rarely tell you which one until after the fact.

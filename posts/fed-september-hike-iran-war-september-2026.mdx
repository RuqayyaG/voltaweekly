The Federal Reserve meets on September 16. Markets are pricing roughly a coin flip between a hold and a 25 basis point hike, with Polymarket sitting at 49% for no change as of this week.

The July meeting set this up. The Fed held at 3.50 to 3.75%, which was expected, but did it on a 9-3 vote with three policymakers dissenting in favour of an immediate hike. That dissent was unusually public. The minutes released August 19 showed broader concern that rates may need to rise if energy-driven inflation keeps feeding into core goods prices.

Here's the problem. The inflation keeping the Fed's hawks vocal isn't being generated by an overheating economy. July CPI came in at 3.4% annually with core at 2.5%. The labour market lost 23,000 jobs in July, well below expectations. On those numbers alone, the case for a hike is thin.

What's complicating it is oil. The Iran war has produced what the IEA called the largest supply disruption in the history of the global oil market. Brent is trading around $90 to $97 a barrel. Every truck moving goods from a warehouse to a shelf is burning more expensive diesel than it was six months ago. That cost doesn't stay in the energy sector. It bleeds into the price of everything that moves.

## What the Fed is actually weighing

Hiking into a supply shock is bad monetary policy by most textbook definitions. Rate hikes reduce demand. They don't fix a closed strait. Raising borrowing costs when American consumers are already squeezed by $4.50 a gallon petrol risks tipping a weakening labour market into something worse.

But the Fed has a credibility problem. It was late in 2021. It was still calling inflation transitory when it wasn't. Kevin Warsh took over as chair and has been more hawkish in tone than Powell. The three dissenters in July were not fringe voices. Letting inflation expectations drift because hiking feels politically awkward during a war is exactly the kind of mistake you correct at higher cost later.

J.P. Morgan Wealth Management now has a September hike as their base case. Cleveland Fed President Beth Hammack, one of the dissenters, has said a 25 basis point move doesn't do much but that it signals intent.

The September decision will probably come down to the jobs data between now and the 16th. A weak payrolls print and they hold. A stable or better print and the dissenters carry the room.

Either way, the Fed is navigating monetary policy in a situation where the thing driving prices up is happening six thousand miles away and nobody in Washington has a clear timeline for when it ends.

Scott Bessent met Chinese Vice Premier He Lifeng at JPMorgan Chase headquarters in New York on September 20. The meeting was billed as preparatory groundwork for a Trump-Xi summit in Washington. What came out of it was a two-month extension of the Busan trade truce, pushing the expiry to January 10.

The Busan agreement, struck in October 2025, suspended Washington's Affiliates Rule in exchange for Chinese exports of rare earths and critical minerals continuing to flow. It was supposed to be the foundation for something more durable. Eight months later, both sides are still negotiating what that looks like.

The two-month extension tells you something. China wanted to extend through the end of the Trump administration. The US wanted a few months to maintain leverage. Scott Kennedy at CSIS told Euronews that the sides would probably land at about a year. They landed at two months. That's shorter than either side publicly wanted.

## What was on the table

The commercial substance goes beyond the truce itself. China was anticipating an announcement of around $30 billion in duty-free or low-tariff imports in return for fulfilling purchase commitments made in May. Washington believed it would extract further purchases in return for that concession.

Deals on agriculture and aircraft were expected. Boeing featured in pre-summit speculation. Chinese retail sales grew just 0.4% in August while fixed-asset investment contracted 7.2% in the first eight months of 2026 compared to the same period last year. Beijing needs the export relationship more than it wants to admit publicly.

Trump's position is also weaker than it looks. Rising gas prices tied to the Iran war have pushed his approval ratings on economic management to new lows heading into midterms. He needs wins from the summit more than he needs leverage.

## Why the short extension matters

A two-month extension means this negotiation is alive again in December. The rare earth export controls that China threatened earlier in the year are still on the table as leverage. US semiconductor export controls on Chinese firms haven't gone away either. The list of unresolved issues is long.

The truce was always a way for both sides to avoid a fight neither could afford. That logic still applies. But extending by only two months means Washington and Beijing will be doing this again at the start of next year, in a different political context, with midterm results in hand and whoever controls Congress shaping what kind of deal is possible.

The summit gets framed as a success. The underlying tensions don't go anywhere.

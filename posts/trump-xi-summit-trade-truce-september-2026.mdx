The US midterm elections are on November 3. All 435 House seats and 35 Senate seats are on the ballot, along with 36 governors. Republicans hold 218 House seats to Democrats' 214. Democrats need a net gain of three to take the majority. The New York Times forecast gives Democrats roughly a seven-in-ten chance. Polymarket has Democrats in the low-to-mid 80s. Polls and prediction markets are pointing the same direction.

The Senate is a different story. Republicans hold 53 seats and Democrats need four to flip it. The structural map favours Republicans, who are defending fewer competitive seats. Polymarket has Republicans in the mid-50s for Senate control. The most likely outcome, on current markets, is Democrats taking the House and Republicans holding the Senate.

Equity markets haven't fully priced that scenario, and it matters more than the headline volatility around election night.

## A Democratic House changes Trump's options immediately

The Iran war has been managed largely through executive authority. Democrats with the House can force a War Powers Resolution vote requiring congressional approval for continued military operations. Whether it passes or has any practical effect is a separate question. It changes the political calculus regardless.

On trade, the tariff architecture is mostly executive order driven and a Democratic House can't repeal it. But it can hold up trade deals requiring congressional ratification. The Busan truce extension runs until January 10. Any permanent deal will need some form of congressional sign-off. A hostile House makes that harder.

Fiscal policy is where the split-Congress scenario does the most damage to Trump's agenda. The 2017 tax cuts passed with a unified Congress. Extensions need the same. A Democratic House will not pass additional tax cuts. Goldman Sachs estimates the fiscal impact of divided government at around 0.3 to 0.5 percentage points of GDP growth relative to unified Republican control.

## Two trades that aren't fully priced

Defence stocks have rallied on the Iran war. A Democratic House with War Powers pressure on the White House introduces a political ceiling on how long the military engagement continues. Names that have priced in multi-year elevated defence spending are exposed to that scenario in a way that isn't currently in the price.

The dollar has benefited from US exceptionalism: higher rates, a government acting aggressively in energy markets, trade policy protecting domestic manufacturers. Divided government that can't pass further fiscal stimulus and faces more scrutiny on the war changes some of those inputs at the margin.

Two weeks out, the midterm trade is still underpriced relative to what's at stake. The specific scenario of Democrats taking the House and Republicans holding the Senate is the modal outcome on prediction markets and it has real consequences for the biggest trades of 2026.

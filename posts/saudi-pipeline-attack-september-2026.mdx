For six months, the global oil market had one story: the Strait of Hormuz was effectively closed, Saudi Arabia was routing crude through its East-West pipeline to the Red Sea port of Yanbu instead, and the world was managing. Painful, but manageable.

On September 10 that contingency ended. Drones launched from Iraqi territory hit pumping stations along the East-West pipeline in the Riyadh and Medina regions. Saudi Arabia shut it down as a precaution the following day. As of now, there is no restart timeline. Brent crude, which had been trading around $90 to $97 before the attack, surged above $107. WTI followed. Oil is up roughly 22% in September alone.

The pipeline is 1,200 kilometres long and runs from Abqaiq on the Gulf coast to Yanbu on the Red Sea, entirely bypassing the Strait of Hormuz. It was the workaround. Without it, Saudi Arabia has no meaningful alternative export route while the Strait remains closed. Al Jazeera reported that analysts said there are no options that can readily compensate for the loss.

## Who did it and why it matters for the wider conflict

Saudi Arabia's Ministry of Foreign Affairs said the drones came from Iraq. Iraq's government condemned the attack and dismissed a military commander in Maysan province. The attribution points toward Iran-aligned militias operating inside Iraqi territory, though no group has claimed responsibility publicly.

This is the strategic logic: if the US-Israel campaign against Iran is going to disrupt Iranian oil exports, Iran and its aligned groups have an interest in disrupting everyone else's too. Closing the Strait hurts Iran but it also hurts every country that ships through it. Taking out Saudi Arabia's bypass route removes the last pressure valve.

The Houthis in Yemen have also been running a parallel campaign. They declared a blockade of Red Sea shipping in July and have been attacking Saudi infrastructure inside the kingdom. The convergence of Houthi strikes from the south and militia drone attacks from the north is not accidental. It's coordinated pressure on Saudi Arabia's ability to export at all.

## What the market is pricing

Brent at $107 with the 10-year Treasury yield near 5% is an uncomfortable combination. The Fed is meeting today. The CME FedWatch Tool had the probability of a 25 basis point hike at 92.7% going into the meeting. The energy shock is now the primary inflation input, not domestic demand.

The White House is reportedly weighing the Defense Production Act as US refineries are running at maximum capacity. That's a domestic supply response to an externally generated problem, which captures the bind policymakers are in everywhere.

The pipeline can theoretically be repaired. Satellite imagery from Vantor showed extensive fire damage at pumping stations but the pipeline itself may not have been directly hit. The question is whether it can be repaired safely while militia attacks from Iraq remain active and while the Houthis continue their campaign.

The market spent six months adjusting to Hormuz being closed. It had no pricing model for Hormuz being closed and the East-West pipeline being shut simultaneously.

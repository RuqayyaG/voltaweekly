The Bank of England held Bank Rate at 3.75% on September 17. The vote was 6-3, with Greene, Mann and Pill voting for a 25 basis point increase to 4%. UK CPI came in at 3.1% in August. Before the Iran war started, the Bank had expected inflation to fall to around 2% from April and stay there through the rest of 2026. That projection is gone.

The hold was expected. Markets had been pricing a 76% probability of no change going into the meeting. But the 6-3 split is more significant than the outcome. Three members voting to hike isn't a fringe position. It's a signal that the committee is moving in a direction, and a November hike is now widely anticipated.

What makes the UK's position unusual right now is that the gilt market is running ahead of the MPC. Yields on 30-year gilts were approaching 6% in the days before the decision. The 10-year benchmark yield dropped 8 basis points after the hold was announced, to 5.21%, which tells you the market had been pricing some chance of a hike. It also tells you that at 5.21% after the relief rally, long-dated UK borrowing costs are still close to multi-year highs.

## Why gilt yields matter beyond monetary policy

Britain has the highest borrowing costs in the G7 right now. That's not just a central bank problem. It's a fiscal problem. The government's debt interest bill rises with gilt yields. The OBR's forecasts were built on assumptions about borrowing costs that no longer hold. Whatever fiscal headroom existed at the spring statement has narrowed.

The Bank is separately planning to reduce its monetary policy gilt holdings to zero, selling around £20 billion annually through active sales on top of maturing gilts. That programme is now running into a market where yields are already elevated. Every billion the Bank sells adds marginal supply pressure to a gilt market that's already under stress from energy-driven inflation concerns and global bond market moves.

## The bind Bailey is in

Andrew Bailey voted to hold. So did five other members. The reasoning is that domestic economic slack and tight financial conditions are already doing disinflationary work. Unemployment hit 4.9% in the three months to July. Private-sector wage growth has been easing. On those measures, the case for holding has logic.

The problem is that energy prices don't care about the domestic labour market. Oil at over $100 a barrel feeds into the inflation print regardless of what's happening to wages. The three dissenters argued that second-round effects, rising goods prices caused by higher energy costs flowing through supply chains, warranted a proactive move now rather than waiting for those effects to show up in the data and then reacting.

The Fed hiked on September 16. The ECB hiked on September 10. The Bank of England held on September 17. It's now the outlier among major central banks in a world where energy-driven inflation is the shared problem. November is live.

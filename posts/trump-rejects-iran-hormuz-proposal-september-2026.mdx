On Friday, Iranian Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi stood at the UN General Assembly in New York and offered a seven-day countdown to reopening the Strait of Hormuz. The conditions were specific: end the US naval blockade of Iranian ports, lift oil sanctions, restore access to frozen Iranian assets, and end Israel's military campaign in southern Lebanon. If the US accepted, Araghchi said the strait could be open within a week and broader nuclear talks could resume.

Trump rejected it on Saturday. "They made a proposal, but I rejected it," he told reporters at the White House. The Wall Street Journal reported, citing US officials, that Trump has told aides he views resumed bombing of Iran after the November midterm elections as likely.

Brent crude had been falling through the week as markets priced in the possibility of a deal. It dropped 2.1% on Friday to settle at $104.32 as UNGA diplomacy generated optimism. After the rejection, futures pointed higher heading into Monday's open. WTI ended the week at $92, down about 7% on the week's peace speculation, before recovering overnight.

## Why Trump said no

The public explanation is that Iran's terms were unacceptable. The private picture, based on the WSJ reporting, is that Trump sees the war as something he wants to conclude on his terms after the midterms, not before. Rejecting a deal now preserves leverage. Accepting Iran's terms would hand Tehran a victory framed around conditions it set unilaterally.

There's also a domestic political calculation. Trump told the UN General Assembly that he would either make a deal with Iran or "annihilate them." Accepting a conditional proposal transmitted through Qatari mediators within days of that speech would read as exactly the kind of capitulation the base would punish.

The problem is the cost of waiting. Iran's economy is under severe pressure, but so is everyone else's. The IEA described this conflict as the largest energy supply disruption in history. US households are estimated to have absorbed around $1,700 in additional costs through higher oil prices and rate hikes. Gas prices are politically toxic. Trump's approval rating on economic management has hit new lows.

## What this means for the market

The rejection removes the near-term resolution scenario the bond and equity markets had been tentatively pricing. Oil's fall through the week was driven partly by deal hopes. Those hopes are now gone, at least until after November's midterms.

The more significant market read is what the WSJ's reporting implies about the timeline. If Trump genuinely plans to resume bombing after the midterms, the Strait of Hormuz disruption is not ending in October. It may not end in November either. Any investment thesis built around a near-term normalisation of energy supply needs to be rethought.

Iran's foreign minister said Tehran would not back down from its conditions. Trump rejected the proposal. There is currently no third party proposing something both sides could accept. The strait stays closed.

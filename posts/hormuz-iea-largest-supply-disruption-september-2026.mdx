Six months ago the US and Israel launched strikes on Iran. The Strait of Hormuz, through which roughly 20% of the world's oil trade passes, has been effectively closed for most of that period. The IEA called it the largest supply disruption in the history of the global oil market.

Brent crude is trading between $90 and $97 a barrel. Gold has pushed above $4,400 an ounce. The 10-year Treasury yield is at 4.78%. On September 1, when US-Iran attacks resumed and two tankers were struck in the Strait, South Korea's KOSPI fell 4% and the Nikkei dropped 2.85%.

And yet equity markets have largely absorbed it. The S&P is not far from its highs. The consensus has been that the disruption is severe but containable, that diplomatic off-ramps exist, and that supply from elsewhere can partially offset the shortfall.

That consensus has been tested repeatedly. So far it has held.

## What history says

The pattern from previous conflicts is actually somewhat reassuring. After the 1990 Gulf War, oil prices spiked and returned to pre-crisis levels within roughly nine months. The same happened after the 2003 Iraq invasion. Brent's rise to nearly $130 after Russia's 2022 invasion of Ukraine eventually reversed too. Markets that panic at the start of a supply shock often recover as alternative routes develop and demand adjusts.

The problem this time is scale and duration. This is not a spike caused by uncertainty about what might happen. The Strait has been disrupted for six months. Vietnam has seen fuel shortages and panic buying. The Philippines declared an energy crisis. Countries that import the majority of their fuel from the Persian Gulf have been scrambling for alternatives at higher cost ever since.

The EIA is forecasting that oil demand will actually decline in 2026 due to high prices and reduced availability, with a rebound expected in 2027 alongside increased US LNG exports. That demand destruction caps how far prices go. It is also a tax on global growth that compounds the longer this drags on.

## The divergence worth watching

Gold at $4,400 is pricing serious tail risk. The 10-year yield at 4.78% reflects an inflation premium that equity markets seem to be ignoring. Brazil's Ibovespa is up on higher oil revenues. Resource-rich countries are winning. Import-dependent economies are losing. The clean story of a unified market reaction to geopolitical risk has broken down.

The September Fed meeting adds another layer. If the Fed hikes on September 16 into an already weakening labour market, that combination of tighter monetary policy and an energy supply shock is the one that historically breaks things in unexpected ways.

Markets have been right to stay calm so far. Whether they are still right in six months is a different question.
